1. Recall from Lecture 5: No Closed-Form MLE

Consider a random sample \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,1)\), with unknown shape \(\alpha>0\) and unit rate. Its density is

\[ f(x\mid\alpha)=\frac{x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}}{\Gamma(\alpha)},\qquad x>0. \]

The likelihood, log-likelihood, and score are, respectively,

\[ \begin{aligned} L(\alpha\mid\mathbf x)&=\frac{e^{-\sum_i x_i}}{\Gamma(\alpha)^n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_i^{\alpha-1},\\ \ell(\alpha)&=-n\log\Gamma(\alpha)+(\alpha-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log x_i-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i,\\ \ell'(\alpha)&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log x_i-n\psi(\alpha), \end{aligned} \]

where \(\psi(\alpha)=\frac{d}{d\alpha}\log\Gamma(\alpha)\) is the digamma function. A stationary point must satisfy

\[\boxed{\psi(\alpha)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log x_i.}\]

The second derivative is

\[\ell''(\alpha)=-n\psi_1(\alpha)<0,\quad \alpha>0,\]

because the trigamma function \(\psi_1(\alpha)=\psi'(\alpha)\) is positive. Thus the log-likelihood is strictly concave; its unique maximum exists for positive observations, but the score equation has no elementary closed-form solution.

Key distinction
Having a unique optimal parameter is not the same as having a formula for it. This is why numerical optimization is needed.

2. From an Analytic Answer to an Iterative Algorithm

Given an objective \(f(\theta)\), our goal might be \(\theta^\star\in\arg\max_\theta f(\theta)\) (a log-likelihood) or \(\theta^\star\in\arg\min_\theta f(\theta)\) (a loss or negative log-likelihood).

When the stationary equation \(f'(\theta)=0\) cannot be solved analytically, a numerical method starts at a guess \(\theta_0\) and forms

\[\theta_0,\theta_1,\theta_2,\ldots\]

using a repeatable update. Good algorithms seek a stationary point while respecting the domain and, when possible, improving the objective.

What we already knowWhat we want to find
\(f(\theta),\ f'(\theta),\ f''(\theta)\)A numerical approximation to a stationary point
Concavity / convexityWhether a stationary point is globally optimal
Starting value \(\theta_0\)An iteration that approaches a solution
A necessary qualification
Newton's method is a local iterative method. Even a strictly concave function does not, by concavity alone, guarantee that the undamped Newton iteration converges from every starting value. We will see how to safeguard it.

3. Deriving Newton–Raphson from Taylor's Formula

3.1 Apply Newton's method to the score equation

At an interior optimum \(\theta^\star\), we expect \(f'(\theta^\star)=0\). Approximate the derivative at \(\theta^\star\) by a first-order Taylor expansion around the current iterate \(\theta_k\):

\[\underbrace{f'(\theta^\star)}_{=0}\ \approx\ f'(\theta_k)+(\theta^\star-\theta_k)f''(\theta_k).\]

Solving the linear approximation for the unknown root gives

\[\theta^\star\approx\theta_k-\frac{f'(\theta_k)}{f''(\theta_k)}.\]
\[\boxed{\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\frac{f'(\theta_k)}{f''(\theta_k)}.}\]

Newton–Raphson iteration in one dimension.

Each step uses both the slope and the curvature at the current parameter value. Geometrically, it finds the zero of a tangent line to the derivative \(f'\).

3.2 Why it moves toward a maximum—locally

Suppose near the current point the objective is concave, so \(f''(\theta_k)<0\). If \(f'(\theta_k)>0\), the Newton correction \(-f'/f''\) is positive and the parameter moves right; if \(f'(\theta_k)<0\), it moves left. Near a strict maximum this tends to move toward the optimum.

Newton solves a root equation
The same update works algebraically for maximization and minimization: it solves \(f'(\theta)=0\). Whether the stationary point is a maximum, minimum, or neither depends on the curvature and the objective. The formula itself does not enforce maximization.

3.3 A few iterations on a simple example

Take \(f(\theta)=-(\theta-3)^2\). Its derivatives are \(f'(\theta)=-2(\theta-3)\) and \(f''(\theta)=-2\). Starting at \(\theta_0=0\),

\[\theta_1=0-\frac{6}{-2}=3.\]

The quadratic reaches its maximum in one step. For a nonquadratic likelihood, several iterations may be necessary.

3.4 When do we stop?

A simple stopping rule checks whether successive iterates are close:

\[|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k|\le\varepsilon(1+|\theta_k|).\]

In practice, also monitor \(|f'(\theta_k)|\), enforce a maximum number of iterations, and guard against near-zero curvature or leaving the admissible parameter space.

4. Worked Example: Newton–Raphson for the Gamma Shape MLE

Return to the Gamma log-likelihood from Section 1. Its first two derivatives are

\[ \boxed{\ell'(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log x_i-n\psi(\alpha),\qquad \ell''(\alpha)=-n\psi_1(\alpha).} \]

Putting these into Newton's formula,

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_{k+1} &=\alpha_k-\frac{\sum_i\log x_i-n\psi(\alpha_k)}{-n\psi_1(\alpha_k)}\\ &=\alpha_k+\frac{\frac1n\sum_i\log x_i-\psi(\alpha_k)}{\psi_1(\alpha_k)}. \end{aligned} \]
Practical starting value
Since \(E[X]=\alpha\) for a Gamma distribution with shape \(\alpha\) and unit rate, the sample mean \(\bar x\) is a reasonable initial guess. In the original R exercise, \(\alpha_0=2\) is chosen to make the iterative path visible.

Because the parameter must remain positive, an overly large Newton step may be invalid. One simple safeguard is step halving: try the full step, then halve it until the candidate is positive and the log-likelihood does not decrease.

For teaching, compare two samples generated under \(\alpha_{\mathrm{true}}=5\): a small sample (\(n=10\)) and a larger sample (\(n=1000\)). Both may yield converged numerical MLEs, but the small-sample MLE can be farther from the generating value purely because of sampling variation.

6. Worked Example: Cauchy Location Likelihood

Now consider a distribution whose likelihood need not be concave. Let \(X_i\) be independent Cauchy observations with location \(\mu\in\mathbb R\) and known scale 1:

\[f(x\mid\mu)=\frac{1}{\pi\{1+(x-\mu)^2\}}.\]

The log-likelihood is

\[\ell(\mu)=-n\log\pi-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[1+(x_i-\mu)^2\right].\]

6.1 Compute the score and curvature

Using the chain rule,

\[\boxed{\ell'(\mu)=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_i-\mu}{1+(x_i-\mu)^2}.}\]

Differentiating again,

\[\boxed{\ell''(\mu)=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2-1}{\left[1+(x_i-\mu)^2\right]^2}.}\]

Unlike Gamma, this curvature can be positive or negative depending on the observations and current location. The log-likelihood is therefore not guaranteed to be globally concave.

6.2 The Newton iteration

\[\boxed{\mu_{k+1}=\mu_k-\frac{\ell'(\mu_k)}{\ell''(\mu_k)}.}\]

The sample median is a sensible initial guess. Unlike the ordinary mean, the theoretical mean of a Cauchy random variable is undefined, and an extreme observation can destabilize the sample mean.

Key lesson: stationary does not mean globally optimal
A Cauchy likelihood may have several stationary points. Newton's method can converge to different local maxima, converge to a local minimum, or take an enormous step if the curvature is near zero. The starting value matters.

8. Newton–Raphson in Higher Dimensions

Suppose now that \(f:\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R\) is twice differentiable and \(\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathbb R^p\). Write its gradient and Hessian as

\[ \nabla f(\boldsymbol\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\partial f/\partial\theta_1\\\vdots\\\partial f/\partial\theta_p\end{pmatrix},\qquad \nabla^2 f(\boldsymbol\theta)=\left[\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial\theta_i\partial\theta_j}\right]_{i,j=1}^p. \]

At an interior stationary point, \(\nabla f(\boldsymbol\theta^\star)=0\). Apply the multivariate Taylor approximation to the gradient:

\[ \underbrace{\nabla f(\boldsymbol\theta^\star)}_{\mathbf0}\approx\nabla f(\boldsymbol\theta_k)+\nabla^2f(\boldsymbol\theta_k)(\boldsymbol\theta^\star-\boldsymbol\theta_k). \]

Define the Newton direction \(\mathbf d_k\) by solving the linear system

\[\boxed{\nabla^2 f(\boldsymbol\theta_k)\mathbf d_k=-\nabla f(\boldsymbol\theta_k).}\]

The update is

\[\boxed{\boldsymbol\theta_{k+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_k+\mathbf d_k.}\]

If the Hessian is invertible, this is formally

\[\boldsymbol\theta_{k+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_k-\left[\nabla^2f(\boldsymbol\theta_k)\right]^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol\theta_k).\]
In actual Python, solve the system
Use np.linalg.solve(H, grad); do not explicitly compute np.linalg.inv(H) @ grad. Solving the linear system is typically more stable and efficient.

For minimization, a positive-definite Hessian makes \(-H^{-1}\nabla f\) a strict descent direction whenever the gradient is nonzero. For maximization, a negative-definite Hessian gives an ascent direction. If the Hessian is indefinite or singular, an unmodified Newton step may be unreliable.

9. Ridge Regression: Why Newton Needs Only One Step

We revisit the ridge objective introduced in Lecture 5:

\[\boxed{Q(\boldsymbol\beta)=\frac12\|\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2,\qquad\lambda>0.}\]

Its derivatives are

\[\nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta)=(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)\boldsymbol\beta-X^\top\mathbf y,\qquad \nabla^2Q=X^\top X+\lambda I_p.\]

Substituting in Newton's iteration gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\beta_{k+1} &=\boldsymbol\beta_k-(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)^{-1}\left[(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)\boldsymbol\beta_k-X^\top\mathbf y\right]\\ &=\boxed{(X^\top X+\lambda I_p)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y=\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\mathrm{ridge}}}. \end{aligned} \]
Why this is a special case
Ridge is quadratic and its Hessian is constant and positive definite. Newton's quadratic approximation is therefore exact. With a full Newton step, the global minimizer is reached in a single iteration from any starting coefficient vector (in exact arithmetic).

"""MATH-224 Lecture 6: Newton-Raphson for likelihood optimization.


Notes:
  * Newton's method does not generally have global-convergence guarantees.
  * A convergence check on the increment alone does not prove a global optimum.
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.special import digamma, polygamma, gammaln

# ----------------------------------------------------------
# 1. GAMMA(shape=alpha, rate=1) likelihood
# ----------------------------------------------------------
def gamma_ll(a: float, data: np.ndarray) -> float:
    if a <= 0:
        return -np.inf
    n = len(data)
    return (a - 1.0) * np.log(data).sum() - data.sum() - n * gammaln(a)


def gamma_score(a: float, data: np.ndarray) -> float:
    return float(np.log(data).sum() - len(data) * digamma(a))


def gamma_hessian(a: float, data: np.ndarray) -> float:
    return float(-len(data) * polygamma(1, a))


def gamma_newton(data: np.ndarray, a0: float = 2.0,
                 tol: float = 1e-9, max_iter: int = 60,
                 safeguard: bool = True) -> tuple[np.ndarray, str]:
    """Newton iteration with optional step-halving to keep a>0 and improve ll."""
    if a0 <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Initial shape must be positive")
    path = [float(a0)]
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        a = path[-1]
        score, hess = gamma_score(a, data), gamma_hessian(a, data)
        step = -score / hess
        scale = 1.0
        new_a = a + step
        if safeguard:
            for _ in range(60):
                if new_a > 0 and np.isfinite(new_a) and gamma_ll(new_a, data) >= gamma_ll(a, data) - 1e-10:
                    break
                scale *= 0.5
                new_a = a + scale * step
            else:
                return np.asarray(path), "line search failed"
        elif new_a <= 0 or not np.isfinite(new_a):
            return np.asarray(path), "left the positive parameter domain"
        path.append(float(new_a))
        if abs(new_a - a) <= tol * (1 + abs(a)):
            return np.asarray(path), "step tolerance reached"
    return np.asarray(path), "maximum iterations reached"


def plot_gamma_samples() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(100)
    true_alpha = 5.0
    # Same demonstration as the R notes, with NumPy's RNG.
    datasets = {10: rng.gamma(shape=true_alpha, scale=1.0, size=10),
                1000: rng.gamma(shape=true_alpha, scale=1.0, size=1000)}
    for n, data in datasets.items():
        path, status = gamma_newton(data, a0=2.0, safeguard=True)
        grid = np.linspace(0.3, 10.0, 400)  # start above zero: Gamma shape > 0
        ll_per_obs = np.array([gamma_ll(a, data) / n for a in grid])
        plt.figure(figsize=(7.6, 4.7))
        plt.plot(grid, ll_per_obs, label="Log-likelihood / n")
        plt.axvline(true_alpha, color="red", ls="--", label="Generating shape")
        plt.axvline(path[-1], color="blue", ls="--", label="Computed MLE")
        plt.plot(path, [gamma_ll(a, data) / n for a in path],
                 "o-", ms=4, label="Newton iterates")
        plt.xlabel(r"Shape $\alpha$")
        plt.ylabel(r"$\ell(\alpha)/n$")
        plt.title(f"Gamma likelihood: n={n}")
        plt.legend()
        plt.tight_layout()
        plt.show()
        print(f"Gamma n={n}: shape truth={true_alpha:.3f}, "
              f"MLE={path[-1]:.6f}, iterations={len(path)-1}, {status}")


# ----------------------------------------------------------
# 2. CAUCHY(location=mu, scale=1) likelihood
# ----------------------------------------------------------
def cauchy_ll(mu: float, data: np.ndarray) -> float:
    r = data - mu
    return float(-len(data) * np.log(np.pi) - np.log1p(r * r).sum())


def cauchy_score(mu: float, data: np.ndarray) -> float:
    r = data - mu
    return float((2.0 * r / (1.0 + r * r)).sum())


def cauchy_hessian(mu: float, data: np.ndarray) -> float:
    r = data - mu
    return float((2.0 * (r * r - 1.0) / (1.0 + r * r) ** 2).sum())


def cauchy_newton(data: np.ndarray, mu0: float,
                  tol: float = 1e-8, max_iter: int = 40) -> tuple[np.ndarray, str]:
    """Raw Newton steps: deliberately show bad starts and nonconcavity."""
    path = [float(mu0)]
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        mu = path[-1]
        score, hess = cauchy_score(mu, data), cauchy_hessian(mu, data)
        if abs(hess) < 1e-9:
            return np.asarray(path), "near-zero curvature (unsafe step)"
        next_mu = mu - score / hess
        if not np.isfinite(next_mu) or abs(next_mu) > 1e7:
            return np.asarray(path), "divergence / numerical overflow"
        path.append(float(next_mu))
        if abs(next_mu - mu) < tol:
            kind = "local maximum" if cauchy_hessian(next_mu, data) < 0 else "not a local maximum"
            return np.asarray(path), f"step tolerance reached; {kind}"
    return np.asarray(path), "maximum iterations reached"


def plot_cauchy_examples() -> None:
    for seed in [1, 10, 33]:
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        data = 5 + rng.standard_cauchy(size=4)
        grid = np.linspace(-10, 40, 1000)
        vals = np.array([cauchy_ll(mu, data) for mu in grid])
        plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4.8))
        plt.plot(grid, vals, color="black", label="Cauchy log-likelihood")
        for mu0, color, label in [(float(np.median(data)), "blue", "sample median"),
                                  (7.0, "red", "start = 7"),
                                  (19.0, "green", "start = 19")]:
            path, status = cauchy_newton(data, mu0)
            valid = path[(path >= grid[0]) & (path <= grid[-1])]
            plt.plot(valid, [cauchy_ll(mu, data) for mu in valid],
                     "o-", color=color, label=f"{label}: {status}", alpha=.85)
        plt.axvline(5, color="gray", ls="--", label="Generating location")
        plt.xlabel(r"Location $\mu$")
        plt.ylabel("Log-likelihood")
        plt.title(f"Cauchy likelihood, n=4, NumPy seed={seed}")
        plt.legend(fontsize=8)
        plt.tight_layout()
        plt.show()
        print(f"Cauchy seed={seed}, data={np.round(data, 3)}")


# ----------------------------------------------------------
# 3. RIDGE REGRESSION: one exact Newton step
# ----------------------------------------------------------
def ridge_newton_example() -> None:
    X = np.array([[1.0, 0.0], [1.0, 1.0],
                  [1.0, 2.0], [1.0, 3.0]])
    y = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 2.0, 4.0])
    lam = 1.5  # includes penalty on both coefficients in this demonstration
    start = np.array([-1.0, 4.0])
    H = X.T @ X + lam * np.eye(X.shape[1])
    grad = H @ start - X.T @ y
    beta1 = start - np.linalg.solve(H, grad)  # use solve, not an explicit inverse
    beta_star = np.linalg.solve(H, X.T @ y)
    print("Ridge beta after 1 Newton step:", beta1)
    print("Ridge analytic minimizer:      ", beta_star)
    print("Difference (numerical rounding):", np.linalg.norm(beta1 - beta_star))
    assert np.allclose(beta1, beta_star)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    plot_gamma_samples()
    plot_cauchy_examples()
    ridge_newton_example()

11. When Newton's Method Can Fail

ProblemWhat can happenPossible remedy
Bad starting valueConverges to an unwanted stationary pointUse a sensible initialization; compare several starts
Very small / zero second derivativeHuge or undefined stepCheck curvature; damp or regularize the step
Leaves the valid parameter domainLikelihood cannot be evaluatedBacktracking / parameter transformation
Nonconcave log-likelihoodDifferent local maxima or even local minimaMultiple starts and objective comparisons
Singular / indefinite multivariate HessianLinear system fails or wrong directionModified Newton methods, line search, trust regions
Stopping only because steps are smallFalse confidence in a solutionCheck gradient, objective, and Hessian signs as well
Correct reading of the convergence statement
Strict concavity guarantees that an existing stationary point is the unique global maximizer. It does not guarantee that raw Newton–Raphson reaches that point from an arbitrary start. With suitable regularity and a starting point sufficiently close to a nonsingular solution, Newton has rapid (typically quadratic) local convergence.

12. Summary, Questions, and Practice

What students should remember

  • Newton's method solves the stationary equation by locally linearizing the derivative.
  • One-dimensional update: \(\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-f'(\theta_k)/f''(\theta_k)\).
  • Multivariate update: solve \(H_kd_k=-g_k\), then set \(\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k+d_k\).
  • Gamma likelihood: strictly concave, unique sample MLE, no elementary closed form.
  • Cauchy likelihood: can be nonconcave, so starting values and curvature matter.
  • Ridge objective: quadratic, so a full Newton step reaches the minimizer exactly.
